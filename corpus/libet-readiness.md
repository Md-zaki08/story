In a series of experiments beginning in the 1980s, neuroscientist Benjamin Libet asked
subjects to perform a simple voluntary action — flexing a wrist or pressing a button —
whenever they felt the urge, while a clock allowed them to report the moment they
became aware of the intention to act. Electroencephalography recorded a "readiness
potential," a slow build-up of brain activity over the motor cortex. The startling
result was that the readiness potential began roughly 300 milliseconds before subjects
reported being conscious of their decision to move. In other words, the brain seemed to
commit to an action before the person knew they had decided.

Libet himself did not conclude that free will was an illusion. He proposed that
consciousness might retain a "veto" — a brief window in which the subject could cancel
an action that had already been prepared. This became known as "free won't." The
experiments have been heavily debated: critics question the timing measures, the
reliability of the readiness potential, the difference between spontaneous and
deliberate action, and whether pressing a button in a lab captures anything like moral
choice. Later work using fMRI and more careful controls produced mixed results, and
some researchers argue the readiness potential reflects general preparation rather than
a specific decision.

The philosophical stakes are large. If conscious will is a late-arriving narrator
rather than an initiating cause, then concepts of responsibility, guilt, and merit
become unstable. The experiment is often used as a laboratory demonstration of
determinism, but its deeper narrative interest lies in the gap between the moment of
action and the moment of ownership: a person can discover that they have already
decided, and must then decide whether to endorse or veto the decision. This structure
— the self as a late-arriving editor of its own impulses — is a powerful story engine.
It also raises questions about machines: if an AI's "decision" is likewise prepared
before its report of deciding, does it have anything like agency, or only the
narration of agency?

Key atoms: the readiness potential that precedes awareness; the veto window; the
clock-and-button method that makes introspection measurable; the late-arriving narrator
of the self; the lab demonstration that turns free will into an empirical question;
and the "free won't" compromise that preserves responsibility by relocating it from
initiation to refusal.
