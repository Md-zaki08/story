Upload is a satirical science-fiction comedy series set in a near future where the
wealthy can digitize their consciousness and live in a virtual afterlife. Nathan Brown,
a young programmer, dies in a suspicious self-driving car crash and is uploaded by his
possessive girlfriend Ingrid into Lakeview, a luxury digital resort run by the
corporation Horizen. In Lakeview, the dead have bodies, appetites, and social lives,
but every experience is monetized: food, weather, upgrades, and even "angels" — living
customer-service representatives who guide the new arrivals — cost money. Nathan is
assigned Nora, a Lakeview employee who is secretly ambivalent about the product she
sells.

The series' engine is the gap between the promise of digital eternity and the
economics of a subscription afterlife. Nathan is a decent, slightly shallow man trying
to understand why he died; Nora is a working-class woman who needs the job but comes to
love him. Their relationship is complicated by the fact that Nora is alive and Nathan
is a paying customer, and by Ingrid's attempts to control Nathan's afterlife. The show
satirizes the gamification of grief, the outsourcing of intimacy to service workers,
and the way digital environments make class hierarchies literal: the rich have
mansions and private beaches, the poor have ads and limited bandwidth. A subplot
involves a hacker who suspects Horizen of killing people to harvest their data, and
the possibility that Nathan's death was not an accident.

The show also explores memory editing: dead users can delete painful memories, and
living users can be manipulated through their digital doubles. Nathan's memories are
incomplete, and the search for what was removed becomes a mystery about identity.
The tone is comedic but the stakes are genuine: love across the boundary of life and
death, the ownership of a person's consciousness, and the question of whether a
digital copy can be wronged.

Narrative atoms include: the subscription afterlife where every comfort is a line item;
the living service worker who falls in love with a dead customer; the possessive
partner who controls the deceased's digital environment; the memory-edit that turns
grief into a product feature; the class-stratified virtual resort; and the death that
may be a data-harvesting crime. The series treats the digital afterlife as a workplace
and a market, not a metaphysical wonder, and its central emotion is the ache of
intimacy that is also a transaction.
