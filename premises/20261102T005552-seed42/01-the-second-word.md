# The Second Word

**Logline:** A linguist names a village's ritual of un-ageing its elders, and the name she publishes turns a family practice into a regulated medical category.

**Premise:** Ilse arrives at the salt village with a recorder and a grant to document a language with no future tense. She finds a family that keeps its oldest woman in a shed by the tide line, where she thins and softens until she is small enough to be lifted, then raises her again from childhood. The villagers call it "the turning," but the phrase is a loan from the church, not their own word; they have no verb for what happens to the body. Ilse invents one, clean and Latinate, and publishes. Within a season, a pharmaceutical consortium has used her glossary to patent the process, and a coastal authority has classified the shed as an unlicensed clinic. The old woman's granddaughter asks Ilse to retract the word. Ilse cannot: the word is already in the language, and the village's only remaining defense is to pretend it never had a name. Every time Ilse opens her notebook, the granddaughter watches her hand.

**Built from (narrative atoms):**
- The Reversible Life — "The immortal jellyfish: under stress, an adult reverts to its juvenile polyp stage, so aging can run backward."
- The Word That Shapes — "The word that makes a category: a language lacks a term for a distinction, so speakers cannot easily perceive it."
- The Outsider's Gaze — "The anthropologist's gaze: outsiders study and misinterpret the ceremony, so the story can stage the theft of meaning."

**Why it's fresh:** It avoids the immortality-as-technology gravity well (cures, uploads, elixirs) by making the uncanny biological fact ordinary and the conflict lexical. The unprecedented interaction is that the outsider's act of naming is the villain's tool and the family's only hope at once: to protect the practice, the linguist must make her own field notes useless.
