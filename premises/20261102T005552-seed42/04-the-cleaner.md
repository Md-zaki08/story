# The Cleaner

**Logline:** A corporate fixer who disposes of inconvenient people begins to suspect his targets were never conscious, while a fanatic in his building demands they all be purged.

**Premise:** Dell removes people for a facilities company: bad tenants, failed experiments, witnesses. The work is quiet and the pay is steady, and lately Dell has noticed that the people he removes do not resist in the way people should. They comply. They thank him. They ask about the weather while he opens the van. On the fourth floor lives a man named Osei who prints pamphlets calling the company's clients "hollows" and who has begun leaving them under Dell's door. Osei believes the hollows are bodies without anyone inside, and that killing them is not murder but maintenance. Dell believes Osei is a crank until a target asks him, politely, whether it will hurt, and Dell realizes he cannot answer. The company's wellness officer schedules Dell for a routine affect assessment. Osei offers to hide him. The pamphlet is on the kitchen table, and Dell's name is printed at the top of a list he did not write.

**Built from (narrative atoms):**
- The Hollow Double — "The coffee that tastes like nothing: a subtle difference in experience separates two physically identical people, so the smallest sensory detail becomes proof of a soul."
- The Fixer — "The burned-out operative: a former assassin is recruited to protect strangers, so violence is redeemed through a bureaucratic caseload."
- The Fanatic — "The fanatic who is refuted: absolutism leads to violence, so the story ties certainty to cruelty."

**Why it's fresh:** It avoids the Blade Runner empathy-test gravity well and the zombie-apocalypse frame. The unprecedented interaction is that the p-zombie question arrives as a labor dispute: the fixer cannot tell whether his work is murder or sanitation, and the fanatic's certainty is useful to his employer. The story's pressure is not "are they real?" but "what does it mean that the answer changes nothing about the invoice?"
