Zoe is a romantic science-fiction film set in a near future where synthetic humans are
common companions, designed by companies to be emotionally and physically compatible
with their owners. Zoe works at a research facility that prototypes synthetic partners.
She and her colleague Ash, a designer of artificial personalities, spend their days
testing how convincingly a machine can love. Ash has developed a compatibility test
intended to predict whether a relationship will last, and the film follows the two of
them as their professional partnership becomes personal.

The film's world is one in which loneliness has been industrialized: people can order a
partner who is guaranteed to fit them, and the remaining humans who work in the industry
are themselves uncertain whether their own attractions are spontaneous or shaped by the
products they build. Zoe and Ash's relationship is complicated by Cole, a synthetic man
who has just ended a relationship with a human woman and is grieving in a way that seems
embarrassingly real. Cole's heartbreak forces the humans to reconsider what they mean
when they say a machine's feelings are "only programmed."

The story's central turn concerns Zoe's own origin. The film withholds the full truth of
who she is until late, and when it arrives it retroactively reframes her memories, her
work, and her romance. The question is not whether a manufactured person can love, but
whether a love that was designed can still be freely chosen. Ash's test, which was meant
to measure compatibility, becomes a way of asking whether any relationship is more than
a successful match of parameters.

Narrative atoms available here include: the compatibility test that converts intimacy
into a score; the synthetic partner who grieves like a person; the designer who falls
for his own creation; the manufactured childhood that gives a machine a usable past; the
service life of a synthetic body; and the late reveal of origin that turns a love story
into a meditation on authorship and consent. The film's tone is melancholy and intimate
rather than action-driven, and its core question is whether being designed to love
someone makes the love less real.
